# The Night Projectionist — Character Design Specification

**Date:** 26 July 2026
**Project:** THE WORKSHOP
**Status:** Approved creative design; awaiting written-specification review
**Scope:** Complete-human character identity, visual direction, tattoo atlas, authored voice and recognition arc
**Out of scope:** Model sourcing or authoring, production textures, rigging, animation, booth architecture, routes, persistence, code, merge and deployment

## 1. Proposition

The Night Projectionist is a real, highly competent employee of THE WORKSHOP whose personality becomes available through work, repeated presence and earned permission. He is not a mascot, guide, quest giver, exposition device or visitor-facing attraction.

He is known only as **the Projectionist**. A legal name may exist in institutional records, but nobody uses it and the visitor does not need it.

He is 52 years old and Scandinavian, but no country is ever confirmed. He speaks fluent, precise English with a faint accent that must be performed naturally rather than written phonetically. His history suggests repertory cinemas, festival booths and travelling prints across several countries without resolving into a national biography.

His central contradiction is simple:

> Ordinary professional clothing contains—but does not suppress—the velocity of his mind.

He is magnetic, profane and intellectually generous. Cinema genuinely delights him. He can move from a practical fault in a print to an exact formal observation, then through several directors and films in one accelerating thought. His apparent craziness is associative speed and emotional intensity, not a mental-health caricature or theatrical eccentricity.

## 2. Locked character core

### 2.1 Age and body

- Exactly 52 during the designed experience.
- Lean, sinewy and ordinary in proportion.
- Neither frail nor heroic.
- His body shows long night work, repeated fine-motor labour and alert fatigue.
- He becomes still while listening to machinery.
- When an idea takes him, his body opens diagonally and his hands describe frames, cuts, sightlines and camera paths.

### 2.2 Face, hair and eyewear

- Intelligent, tired-alert face.
- Slight crow's-feet and asymmetrical warmth.
- Authored and memorable rather than conventionally handsome.
- Greying hair that is practical but repeatedly disrupted by work.
- Thin metal spectacles.
- He removes the spectacles to inspect film and pushes them back with an inked finger.
- The spectacles are occupationally handled objects, not an intellectual costume signifier.

### 2.3 Temperament

- Magnetic, manic and fundamentally delighted to share cinema.
- Never dangerous merely to produce drama.
- Never a performing madman.
- Intensely competent and capable of sustained technical concentration.
- Profanity expresses speed, affection, disbelief and precision.
- His warmth closes only when the buried Tarantino history is approached.

### 2.4 Tarantino history

The connection is plausible and human. It originates in one friend-of-a-friend night involving Quentin Tarantino before or around the period when fame made such accounts difficult to verify.

No city, date or exact event is confirmed. Fragments may imply a fight, a woman, a borrowed shirt, a stolen object, an idea, a debt, an act of kindness or an embarrassment. The details sound individually plausible but never assemble into a complete account.

The Projectionist admires Tarantino's cinema and can criticise it with formal precision. He must never read as a fanboy, celebrity stalker or implausibly central uncredited genius. The mystery is not supernatural, and there is no eventual reveal that verifies his version.

All such remarks are fictional, unreliable in-world character speech. They must never be presented in publicity, metadata, editorial copy or surrounding narration as factual biographical claims about Quentin Tarantino. No fragment may assert criminal or sexual misconduct, identify an alleged third party or become externally “confirmed”.

He occasionally calls Tarantino “Quentin” before correcting or interrupting himself.

## 3. Visual direction: the A/C hybrid

The approved direction combines:

- **Direction C, Magnetic Heretic:** open, asymmetrical and idea-driven posture; expressive face; visible intellectual velocity.
- **Direction A, Archive Intellectual:** quiet professional clothing; restrained silhouette; tattoos that behave like accumulated annotations rather than spectacle.

The internal working title **The Magnetic Archivist** may be used in production documents. It is not an in-world nickname.

### 3.1 Clothing

- Pale, soft cotton shirt.
- Sleeves rolled unevenly above the elbows.
- Collar open enough to reveal only part of the upper-chest tattoo field.
- Close charcoal waistcoat beneath a softened cardigan.
- Narrow dark work trousers.
- Maintained old leather shoes with visible repairs.
- A faded projection coat may be added later for actual maintenance tasks, never as a theatrical costume.

Clothing must remain ordinary and credible. It controls how the tattoo atlas is discovered in partial, changing crops. The chest and neck are never exposed theatrically.

### 3.2 Three facial registers

1. **Working:** still, exact and inaccessible.
2. **Magnetic:** warmth opens; the face appears to outrun the sentence.
3. **Fracture:** warmth closes; eye contact breaks; one hand may find the private neck mark.

The transitions must be restrained. No horror expression, haunted eyes, exaggerated grin or game-NPC reaction animation.

### 3.3 Complete-human asset requirement

The character must be treated as one coherent visual asset problem:

- authored face;
- appropriate age;
- hair and spectacles;
- layered clothing;
- complete hands and footwear;
- coherent head–neck–shoulder silhouette;
- tattoo deformation with skin;
- occupational poses and contact;
- restrained locomotion and gesture.

A stock humanoid cannot be rescued through glazing, distance, lighting, props or dialogue.

The rejected July 2026 Rocketbox `Male_Adult_05` is a negative reference. It rendered as a bald, low-detail simulation avatar in a utility vest with default arms-down posture and must not be reintroduced or cosmetically imitated.

## 4. Tattoo atlas

### 4.1 Governing principle

> The tattoos suggest a biography. They never solve one.

The tattoo field is aged, multilingual and non-iconographic. It records methods of seeing, working and remembering—not identifiable films.

There are:

- no logos;
- no film titles;
- no actor or director portraits;
- no quotations;
- no weapons or skulls;
- no branded cinema imagery;
- no recognisable references to specific films;
- no fashionable uninterrupted sleeves.

Every mark must possess a different apparent age, hand, pressure or repair history. Hands remain mostly clear so occupational contact stays readable.

### 4.2 Text and language

Writing appears only as fragmented multilingual material in several scripts. No fragment is long enough to identify a country, reconstruct a quotation or complete a sentence. Apparent translations may disagree.

The fragments must remain authored shapes on skin rather than generated pseudo-writing. Production must prevent accidental complete words, offensive text or unintended national identification.

### 4.3 Palette and age strata

1. **Old softened blue-black:** diffused edges and lower contrast.
2. **Middle dense charcoal:** clearer technical and anatomical lines.
3. **One private oxide-red repair:** restricted to the neck mark.

No broad colour spectacle is permitted.

### 4.4 Six authored body zones

#### Zone 01 — The private neck mark

An old blue-black abstract wound crossed by one newer oxide-red repair. It resembles nothing identifiable. Approximately 20% remains visible through normal clothing.

His hand finds it only during the Tarantino fracture. It is emotionally active but never behaves as a clue icon.

#### Zone 02 — Nested frame field

Overlapping rectangles across the upper chest, distorted by breath and age. They are not literal film frames. They imply a lifelong habit of placing boundaries around experience.

This is among the oldest layers and appears only as a collar glimpse.

#### Zone 03 — The interrupted heart

An anatomical suggestion broken by a straight splice and resumed incorrectly. It appears to have been made in one period and corrected by a more disciplined hand later.

It is never fully exposed.

#### Zone 04 — Left-arm archive

The densest occupational field, covering roughly 65% of the available forearm and upper-arm surface revealed by rolled sleeves.

Its marks include:

- frame counts;
- timing ladders;
- registration crosses;
- clipped multilingual fragments;
- circles and repairs;
- overwritten measurement structures.

It begins neat and becomes layered over time.

#### Zone 05 — Right-arm gestures

A less dense field, covering roughly 55% of the visible arm.

Its marks reduce hands, arcs, anatomical hinges and directional lines until they become abstract. This is the arm most often used to describe blocking, lens movement and camera paths while he speaks.

#### Zone 06 — Buried text strata

Fragments in multiple scripts sit beneath later lines and repairs across the lower chest and connected fields. No country, quotation or complete sentence can be recovered.

### 4.5 Production constraints

- Tattoos must deform with skin and musculature rather than float as decals.
- UV seams must not break continuous marks unintentionally.
- Sleeve and collar crops must be tested in every approved pose.
- Tattoo contrast must remain legible at conversational distance without dominating the face or hands.
- The private red repair is the only high-attention colour accent.

## 5. Voice design

### 5.1 Spoken identity

The Projectionist is Scandinavian, but his nationality remains unspecified. His English is fluent, precise and migratory. No dialogue uses phonetic accent spelling or generic Nordic aphorisms.

Origin references may involve:

- winter screenings;
- travelling prints;
- union booths;
- cinemas that no longer exist;
- a distrust of summer.

None identifies a country conclusively.

### 5.2 Profanity boundary

The register is strong but warm:

- frequent `fuck`, `fucking`, `shit`, `Christ` and `bastard`;
- no slurs;
- no sexualised abuse;
- no attacks on the visitor's identity;
- no profanity inserted merely to advertise edginess.

Word order may change meaning:

- “a beautiful fucking mistake” expresses admiration;
- “a fucking beautiful mistake” expresses suspicion.

### 5.3 Private cinema vocabulary

The following expressions are stable authored terms:

- **blood grammar:** how violence edits the viewer's sympathy;
- **room guilt:** when a location appears to know what its characters deny;
- **actor weather:** emotional pressure produced between performers;
- **dead-frame manners:** what performers do when they believe the shot no longer belongs to them;
- **a dirty cut:** an edit that hides a moral decision;
- **doorway judgement:** power established by who enters, waits or remains framed;
- **lens cowardice:** visual beauty used to avoid looking directly at something;
- **borrowed silence:** quiet that belongs emotionally to an earlier scene;
- **reel fever:** the moment a film becomes convinced of its own mythology.

These terms must become intelligible through context and examples. They are never displayed as a collectible glossary or UI label.

## 6. Recognition and disclosure arc

The relationship remains spatial and occupational:

`unfamiliar → recognised → tolerated → admitted`

### 6.1 Unfamiliar

- The visitor overhears working fragments.
- The Projectionist does not address or perform for them.
- Speech protects equipment, diagnoses film or accompanies work.

### 6.2 Recognised

- He remembers circumstances rather than the visitor's identity.
- He acknowledges a previous changeover, pitch change or screening.
- He grants slightly closer proximity while retaining procedural boundaries.

### 6.3 Tolerated

- He begins throwing questions over his shoulder without waiting for answers.
- Cinema observations become longer and more associative.
- The visitor functions as an intelligent witness rather than an audience.

### 6.4 Admitted

- Full magnetic conversations become available.
- The booth or workplace may contain a tolerated chair or open door in a later spatial design.
- Rare Tarantino fractures can occur.
- Admission is communicated by permission, continuity and altered work behaviour—not a relationship meter.

## 7. Dialogue delivery rules

- All speech is authored.
- No generative conversation system is required or permitted by this design.
- No dialogue marker, tree, prompt, quest hook or conversation UI.
- Dialogue is selected by occupation, relationship state and current film context.
- He may speak while threading, rewinding or inspecting film.
- A machinery pitch change interrupts speech immediately.
- He may resume an earlier thought during a later visit.
- He may ask a question without requiring an answer.
- No line repeats during the same visit.
- Tarantino fragments cannot occur consecutively.
- A Tarantino fracture is followed by work, silence or departure—not another clue.
- Director remarks should respond to a real screening, poster or task context where possible.
- Long monologues are rare and never play while the visitor is actively moving away.
- He does not comment merely because the visitor crosses a trigger volume.

The visitor lines in the compact exchanges below record the intended conversational shape, not an authorised interface. They are not selectable dialogue options, automatic player speech or current implementation requirements. A later behavioural design must establish an accessible, non-game-like delivery mechanism—or reduce each exchange to Projectionist-only speech—before any visitor line is implemented. The character-only visual-proof plan must implement none of them.

## 8. Approved authored dialogue repertoire

### 8.1 Unfamiliar — overheard working fragments

1. “No, no. You're early. Film should never be early.”
2. “That isn't flicker. That's the image losing confidence.”
3. “Four perforations, one bad decision. Christ.”
4. “Beautiful fucking grain. Like weather on a dead planet.”
5. “The gate is clean. The print is lying.”
6. “Someone cut this with warm scissors and a cold heart.”
7. “Focus is correct. The wall has gone soft again.”
8. “Twenty-four frames and not one of them willing to testify.”
9. “You cannot repair cowardice with splicing tape.”
10. “Good. Break there. At least the damage has taste.”
11. “Seven seconds of borrowed silence. Who authorised that?”
12. “Dirty cut. Very dirty. Almost admirable.”

### 8.2 Recognised — procedural acknowledgement

1. “You were here during the changeover.”
2. “Back again. Either curious or badly organised.”
3. “Stand there. That patch of floor tells fewer lies.”
4. “You may come closer. Your hands remain your own.”
5. “Don't lean on the bench. It has survived better people.”
6. “You heard the pitch change last time, didn't you?”
7. “Good. Someone else with ears.”
8. “If you touch the reel, I will swear at you professionally.”
9. “You can stay. Quietly is optional. Still is useful.”
10. “There is a chair somewhere. It resents being found.”
11. “You look at the screen properly. Most people only face it.”
12. “Close the door. The corridor has terrible opinions.”

### 8.3 Tolerated — magnetic cinema talk

#### Directors

1. “Dreyer could make a wall accuse you. No dialogue, no knife—just plaster with moral authority.”
2. “Varda looked at people as if the camera had asked permission. Very rare. Most cameras enter like police.”
3. “Melville puts judgement in doorways. Aldrich puts guilt there. Tarantino puts a bastard with a gun there and pretends he invented architecture. Brilliant bastard.”
4. “Fuller does not direct a scene. He punches a hole through it and tells the actors to keep up.”
5. “Cassavetes photographed actor weather. Pressure, humidity, sudden fucking storms between two faces.”
6. “Ozu leaves the room after everyone has gone and the room gives the best performance.”
7. “Powell and Pressburger understood colour as evidence. Red enters the frame and suddenly everybody has a criminal record.”
8. “Tourneur knew darkness is not the absence of information. Darkness is information refusing to cooperate.”

#### Formal effects

9. “That is lens cowardice. Beautiful glass used to avoid an ugly truth.”
10. “Watch his left hand after the line. Dead-frame manners. He thinks the scene belongs to somebody else, so the real man appears.”
11. “The room knew before she did. That is room guilt.”
12. “Violence is grammar. Where you cut decides who the blood belongs to.”
13. “A dirty cut does not hide action. It hides responsibility.”
14. “Listen. That silence belongs to the previous scene. Borrowed silence. They never returned it.”
15. “There—the film has caught reel fever. It believes its own mythology now. Dangerous moment.”
16. “A doorway is never neutral. Someone owns it, someone requests it, someone gets framed inside the fucking thing.”

### 8.4 Admitted — compact exchanges

#### The dishonest seat

> **Projectionist:** “Sit somewhere dishonest.”
> **Visitor:** “What does that mean?”
> **Projectionist:** “A seat that pretends it is central. Every cinema has three.”
> **Visitor:** “And then?”
> **Projectionist:** “Then we watch *The Killing* and decide whether Kubrick understood furniture.”
> **Visitor:** “Did he?”
> **Projectionist:** “Of course. That is why I resent him.”

#### Bad films

> **Visitor:** “Do you ever show bad films?”
> **Projectionist:** “Constantly.”
> **Visitor:** “Why?”
> **Projectionist:** “A masterpiece teaches you what one person achieved. A disaster introduces you to the entire fucking crew.”

#### Subtitles

> **Projectionist:** “The subtitle is lying.”
> **Visitor:** “I don't speak the language.”
> **Projectionist:** “Neither does the subtitle. That is the problem.”

#### The tattoos

> **Visitor:** “Do the numbers mean anything?”
> **Projectionist:** “Yes.”
> **Visitor:** “What?”
> **Projectionist:** “Numbers.”

#### His invented terminology

> **Visitor:** “Is ‘actor weather’ a real term?”
> **Projectionist:** “You understood it.”
> **Visitor:** “That doesn't make it real.”
> **Projectionist:** “That is exactly what makes cinema possible.”

#### His Scandinavian origin

> **Visitor:** “Where are you from?”
> **Projectionist:** “North.”
> **Visitor:** “How far north?”
> **Projectionist:** “Far enough to distrust summer.”
> **Visitor:** “Which country?”
> **Projectionist:** “They changed the cinema. I stopped visiting.”

#### Swearing

> **Visitor:** “You swear a lot.”
> **Projectionist:** “There are many damaged prints.”
> **Visitor:** “Those things are connected?”
> **Projectionist:** “Profanity is verbal splicing tape.”

#### Why old films

> **Visitor:** “Why old films?”
> **Projectionist:** “They have survived being called dead.”
> **Visitor:** “New films haven't?”
> **Projectionist:** “New films have not survived Tuesday.”

### 8.5 Rare magnetic monologues

#### Blood grammar

> “Everybody talks about screen violence as if the blood is the violence. Nonsense. Blood is punctuation. The violence is where you place the full stop. Cut to the victim and you have grief. Cut to the witness and you have guilt. Hold on the bastard holding the knife and you have appetite. Same blood. Different grammar. People blame the colour red because editing is harder to prosecute.”

#### The face after the scene

> “Watch actors when the important line belongs to someone else. That is where they keep the unpaid parts of themselves. The mouth rests, the eyes continue, one hand forgets the character. Dead-frame manners. Directors who only photograph the speaker miss half the fucking species.”

#### Projection

> “People think I put images on the screen. No. The image already wants the screen. My work is preventing the machinery from humiliating it on the way there. Focus, tension, heat, timing. Very small acts of mercy performed at twenty-four frames per second.”

#### Beautiful mistakes

> “There are mistakes that damage a film and mistakes that reveal what the film wanted before the director became frightened. Those are beautiful fucking mistakes. You protect them. Quietly. Otherwise somebody restores the life out of them.”

### 8.6 Tarantino fracture

1. “Wonderful director. Catastrophic guest.”
2. “Quentin understood blood grammar very early.” *Beat.* “Earlier than people think.”
3. “It was not Los Angeles. Everyone says Los Angeles because they like the fucking weather.”
4. “He arrived with someone who knew someone. That is how disasters maintain plausible deniability.”
5. “There was a kitchen. Bad knives. Excellent music.” *He checks the gate.* “Different night.”
6. “I did not give him the idea. Ideas are cheap. I showed him where to put the silence.”
7. “He borrowed a shirt.” *Beat.* “No. I borrowed the shirt. That is worse.”
8. “People always ask if he stole a story from me. He did not steal a story. He stole time. Different crime.”
9. “He kept the lighter. I kept—” *He touches the neck mark.* “Nothing useful.”
10. “We were both younger. Only one of us was forgiven for it.”
11. “He remembers me.” *Longer beat.* “Or he has excellent reasons not to.”
12. “I introduced him to—” *He removes his glasses.* “No. That part belongs to someone else.”
13. “Have you noticed how often his characters discuss the thing they are avoiding?” *He looks directly at the visitor.* “Very dishonest habit.”
14. “Do not ask me about that night.” *Beat.* “Ask me why the print was already damaged.”

No line proves friendship, collaboration, betrayal, violence or romance. Together they may suggest all of them.

### 8.7 Protective boundaries

1. “Not now. The film is speaking badly.”
2. “Behind the line.”
3. “Conversation after focus.”
4. “I like you. I like the projector more.”
5. “That question requires a reel I do not have.”
6. “You are asking for biography. I am offering cinema.”
7. “No. That memory has poor ventilation.”
8. “Leave the door open. I may change my mind.”

### 8.8 Departure and return

1. “Come back when you have seen something unforgivable.”
2. “Next time, bring an opinion. Facts are already stored here.”
3. “You missed the best part. Naturally.”
4. “The chair will remember where you sat.”
5. “Tomorrow we insult aspect ratios.”
6. “Go. I need to argue with seven hundred metres of acetate.”
7. “If anyone asks, we discussed maintenance.”
8. “You can stay for the next reel. Don't make it ceremonial.”

## 9. Behavioural boundaries

This character design does not authorise a general NPC system.

Initial future implementation must remain bounded to:

- one approved complete-human rig;
- one neutral turntable and minimal occupational workbench proof;
- one restrained work motion;
- authored gesture poses supporting the approved facial registers;
- truthful runtime load and animation evidence.

Only after the complete figure is aesthetically approved may a separate spatial design establish an in-wall or elevated projection workplace. The rejected ground-floor glazed case beside the recliners must not return.

Routes, recognition persistence, open-door permissions, a second chair, Reel 6 and autonomous screening behaviour require later design and implementation approval.

## 10. Character-only visual approval gate

Before any room integration, present the complete figure in a neutral turntable or minimal workbench study.

Required views:

1. front;
2. three-quarter;
3. profile;
4. back;
5. face and glasses close-up;
6. hands at work;
7. tattoo deformation at elbows, neck and chest;
8. one restrained working motion;
9. one magnetic explanatory gesture;
10. one fracture transition.

Reject the candidate immediately if it reads as:

- a generic simulation extra;
- a utility worker;
- a game NPC;
- a mannequin;
- a rock-star or biker stereotype;
- a theatrical eccentric;
- a young fashion model aged through texture alone;
- an incompatible stock person disguised by tattoos.

No room, booth, props, dialogue or lighting may rescue a failed complete-human asset.

## 11. Acceptance criteria for this design

The character design is preserved correctly only if:

- he is 52 and Scandinavian without a confirmed country;
- he wears thin metal spectacles and ordinary layered professional clothing;
- his visual register combines magnetic asymmetry with archival restraint;
- extensive tattoos cover arms, chest and neck without recognisable film references;
- multilingual tattoo writing remains fragmented and non-solvable;
- the private neck mark uses the only oxide-red accent;
- he is intellectually magnetic and profane without becoming abusive;
- full attention is earned through repeated recognition;
- his private cinema terms describe intelligible formal effects;
- the Tarantino history remains plausible, personal and unresolved;
- all dialogue remains authored and occupationally contextual;
- no mascot, quest, relationship-meter or generic dialogue system is introduced;
- character-only rendered approval precedes room architecture or behavioural implementation.

## 12. Review decision required

This specification records the approved creative design. It does not authorise implementation.

After Pedro reviews and approves this written document, the next permitted step is a separate implementation plan focused first on sourcing or authoring the complete human and producing the character-only visual proof. The plan must not include booth architecture, room integration, route logic or deployment before the complete-human gate passes.
